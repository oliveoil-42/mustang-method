02 / YOUR TOOLKIT

A strategy for the situation.

Try one approach for a week. Keep what helps, adjust what doesn’t, and judge success by whether it supports your priorities.

01

When your week feels scattered

Time blocking

Put fixed commitments on one calendar. Add realistic work blocks and transition time. Leave a buffer for unfinished work.

Put it into practice

Try it today: protect one 45-minute block and give it a specific output.

A studio model, a case analysis and a lab report all need different blocks; include setup and cleanup.

02

When starting is the hardest part

The 10-minute start

Choose the smallest visible next action. Work for ten minutes, then decide whether to continue or take a break.

Put it into practice

Try it today: open the prompt and write three questions you need to answer.

Begin a proof with definitions, an essay with a claim, or a coding project with one test case.

03

When everything feels urgent

Priority sorting

Separate deadline pressure from importance. Do urgent, important work first; schedule important work before it becomes urgent. Renegotiate or reduce lower-value commitments.

Put it into practice

Try it today: choose one must-do, one should-do and one optional task.

Compare tomorrow’s pre-lab, next week’s critique and a club request before accepting more work.

04

When a project feels too large

Backward planning

Start at the due date. Add review and revision time, then place smaller milestones backward on your calendar.

Put it into practice

Try it today: give the first rough version a deadline before the final submission.

Use research → outline → draft for a paper; requirements → prototype → test for an engineering build.

05

When interruptions take over

Focused work sprints

Pick one task, put distracting tabs away and work for a short interval. Start with 25 minutes and a 5-minute break; adjust to the task.

Put it into practice

Try it today: write a clear stopping point before starting your timer.

Reading and problem sets can fit short sprints. Fieldwork or experiments may require longer uninterrupted periods.

06

When plans stop matching reality

Weekly reset

Review deadlines, unfinished work and commitments. Estimate the week’s demands, move tasks deliberately and protect recovery time.

Put it into practice

Try it today: spend 15 minutes choosing your three most important outcomes.

Check lab opportunities and research emails, team milestones, reading loads and extracurricular commitments together.

SAME PRINCIPLES. DIFFERENT WEEKS.

Make it work for your major.

Your workload is more than your class schedule. Explore examples across all six colleges, then borrow ideas from any discipline that fits your work.

Count the invisible commitments.

Include lab preparation, research meetings, travel, equipment access, team coordination, paid work and care responsibilities. Before adding an opportunity, ask what time it needs and what you will move to make space.

Interested in research? Keep a list of faculty whose work interests you and check departmental announcements for openings. Hiring cycles vary; confirm dates with the lab.

03 / THE POINT OF MAKING TIME

What are you making room for?

A good week includes more than completed assignments. Let your values shape the calendar: meaningful work, relationships, curiosity, contribution and rest.

Connection

Choose one moment to be fully present with someone. A shared meal or a walk counts.

Who do you want to show up for this week?

Curiosity & contribution

Make room for a creative practice, a club or service that matters to you. Start with a commitment you can sustain.

What would make this week feel meaningful?

Rest & boundaries

Protect time to recharge. Set a stopping time, leave some space unplanned and practice declining commitments when your week is full.
